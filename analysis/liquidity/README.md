# Liquidity: the price of the one-hop restriction

The protocol forbids passing on received backing. Section 6 of
[`docs/CREDIT_MODEL.md`](../../docs/CREDIT_MODEL.md) says multi-hop routing would raise honest
liquidity and keep the Sybil bound. This directory measures both halves of that claim. It compares
one-hop backing with 2-hop, 3-hop and unbounded credit networks and with a centralised lender, on
synthetic social graphs, for a single borrower and for repeated loans. It then checks the Sybil
bound in every regime and asks who pays when a borrower defaults.

```bash
python3 -m unittest discover analysis/liquidity   # 26 tests, about 10 s
python3 analysis/liquidity/run.py                 # every experiment, about 4 minutes on 4 cores
```

Requirements: Python 3.11 with numpy, scipy, matplotlib and networkx. Every task derives its seeds
from its own parameters, so the outputs are identical on every run and for any number of processes.

## Model

**Population.** n accounts on a social graph:

- Watts-Strogatz small world (n = 500 to 2,000, mean degree 4 to 12, rewiring 0.05 to 0.3);
- Barabasi-Albert (m = degree / 2 edges per new node);
- a stochastic block model with 10 equal blocks, 10% of each node's expected degree across blocks.
  It is run twice: with credit anywhere ("credit everywhere"), and with credit only in the first
  five blocks ("credit in half the blocks").

A share q of accounts hold an issued line L = 100 (`maxLoanAmount`); the rest hold none. Holder
sets are nested in q and the graph is shared across q and c, so sweeps compare like with like.

**Trust.** Every edge carries a line of c in each direction: a holder will back each neighbour up to
c, and the total it backs never exceeds its free credit. Lines commit nothing until a loan draws on
them.

**Regimes.** A borrower without credit can draw:

| Regime | Maximum borrowable |
| --- | --- |
| one-hop (deployed rule) | the sum over neighbours of min(c, free credit) |
| k-hop, k = 2, 3 | the max-flow from all holders to the borrower along paths of at most k arcs, arc capacity c, each holder's supply capped by its free credit |
| unbounded | the same over paths of any length |
| centralised | all free credit; any holder lends to anyone |

The supply cap is node splitting: each holder becomes a supply node, with capacity equal to its
issued credit, feeding a relay node with no capacity of its own. Two readings of "node capacity
equal to issued credit" exist, and they give opposite answers, so both are computed:

- **Supply cap** (above). Accounts without credit can relay. This is the credit network of
  Karlan et al. (2009) and Dandekar et al. (2011), and mechanism M4 of `analysis/sybil_sim`.
- **Throughput cap** ("path-liable"). Node splitting on every node, so each account passes on at
  most its own free credit. That is what it takes for every intermediary to be able to pay, on
  chain, for what it passed on.

Two more one-hop variants frame the deployed contract. **one-hop-static** commits at `back` time,
as the contract does: each holder splits its credit evenly over its neighbours without credit
before anyone borrows. Plain one-hop commits on draw, which is the same as calling `back` and
borrowing in one transaction.

**Single borrower.** On a fresh network, a random account without credit. Report P(maximum >= x)
for x = 50 (half a full line) and the mean maximum. 600 borrowers per setting (3 graphs x 200).

**Repeated transactions** (after Dandekar et al. 2011). One request per step, from a uniformly
random account without credit, for x = 50. A granted loan draws its flow and is repaid after a
geometric number of steps, which releases the flow. The mean duration D makes the open principal,
if every request succeeded, a share rho = 50% of all issued credit (Little's law). After a warm-up
of max(3D, 300) requests, 2,000 requests are measured. Standard errors come from 5 batches of 400.
The same request sequence is used for every regime.

A multi-hop regime first tries direct backers, pro rata as one-hop does. Only if they fall short
does it route a longer flow. For k = 2, 3 the route is the k-hop flow with the fewest arc units (an
LP with a small cost per arc). For unbounded it is Dinic's max-flow, which augments along shortest
paths first.

**Sybils.** The attacker adds m accounts with no credit and unlimited trust among themselves. g
distinct honest accounts each extend a line of c to one of them (attack edges). Extraction is the
max-flow into the whole Sybil set from a fresh network. The endpoints are random, credit holders
only, or accounts without credit only.

**Base point.** n = 1,000, Watts-Strogatz, mean degree 8, rewiring 0.1, q = 0.2, L = 100, c = 25
(a full line backs four neighbours), x = 50, rho = 50%.

## Method and checks

- **2-hop is exact and integral.** It is a plain max-flow on a layered network: a supply node per
  holder, a relay node per neighbour of the borrower, and every trust arc at most once. Its
  source-to-sink paths are exactly the paths of one or two arcs.
- **3-hop is an LP.** Length-bounded flow is not a plain max-flow, because an arc can sit at
  different positions of different paths and share its capacity between them. The LP has one
  variable per (arc, position) and a bundle constraint per arc. This is the time-expanded
  formulation of Baier et al. (2010). Its optimum is fractional in general. The integral version is
  NP-hard, so an on-chain router that uses whole units may find slightly less.
- **Unbounded** uses scipy's Dinic. The minimum cut is read off the residual graph, and its
  capacity is summed from the network's own capacities.
- **Tests.** The k-hop LP matches explicit enumeration of every simple path of at most k arcs, for
  single sinks and sink sets. The layered 2-hop matches the LP. Unbounded matches networkx and the
  k-hop LP with k = n - 1. Every flow respects capacities and conserves flow. Cuts equal flows, and
  the 3-hop LP dual equals its primal. Path-liable equals one-hop on random networks with random
  capacities. With instant repayment, the simulation reproduces the single-borrower values exactly.
- **Run-time checks** in `run.py`, on all 25,800 sampled borrowers:
  - one-hop-static <= one-hop <= 2-hop <= 3-hop <= unbounded <= centralised;
  - path-liable = one-hop on 2,580 of them;
  - in all 36 Sybil cases (144 runs), extraction is identical for m = 1, 10, 100 and 1,000,
    equals its regime's cut and is at most g x c in every trust regime.

## Results

### How much liquidity one-hop costs

At the base point ([figure](figures/success_vs_degree.png)):

| | one-hop (commit at `back`) | one-hop | 2-hop | 3-hop | unbounded | centralised |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| P(can borrow 50), fresh network | 0.12 | 0.50 | 0.98 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| Mean maximum borrowable | | 40 | 163 | 198 | 201 | 20,000 |
| Steady-state success (SE <= 0.006) | | 0.41 | 0.83 | 0.96 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| Issued credit in use (offered: 50%) | | 20% | 42% | 48% | 50% | |

Gain over one-hop at the base point, all four graph families
([summary](results/summary.md), section 1):

| Graph | fresh: 2-hop / one-hop | 3-hop / one-hop | steady: one-hop | 2-hop | 3-hop | unbounded | 2-hop / one-hop | 3-hop / one-hop |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Watts-Strogatz | 1.94x | 1.99x | 0.41 | 0.83 | 0.96 | 1.00 | 2.04x | 2.36x |
| Barabasi-Albert | 2.51x | 2.51x | 0.28 | 0.85 | 0.98 | 0.98 | 3.05x | 3.50x |
| SBM, credit everywhere | 2.00x | 2.01x | 0.43 | 0.91 | 0.98 | 0.99 | 2.13x | 2.30x |
| SBM, credit in half the blocks | 2.02x | 2.95x | 0.26 | 0.48 | 0.68 | 0.88 | 1.85x | 2.61x |

- One-hop serves a third to a half of borrowers at a fresh state, and 28 to 43% under repeated
  loans. When credit is spread, every multi-hop regime serves 83 to 100%. So one-hop roughly halves
  honest liquidity at the base point. In the steady state 2-hop multiplies it by 1.85 to 3.05.
- In credit terms ([figure](figures/equivalent_issuance.png)), one-hop needs more issued credit to
  match multi-hop at q = 0.2: 2.1x to match 2-hop, 3.1x to match 3-hop and 3.8x to match
  unbounded (steady state, targets capped at 99%). By Theorem 2 lenders' worst-case loss is the
  issued credit. So the price of the one-hop rule is two to four times the loss bound for the same
  service.
- The price falls as the graph gets denser and credit gets more common (both sweep figures). At
  degree 12, one-hop reaches 0.60 in the steady state against 0.96 for 2-hop. At q = 0.5 it
  reaches 0.68 against 0.89. The price rises when credit is scarce: at q = 0.05, 0.04 against
  0.55 (2-hop) and 0.85 (3-hop).
- c matters most. With c >= 50 one backer covers the loan, and one-hop reaches 0.80 against 1.00.
  With c = 10 a loan of 50 needs five holder neighbours, and one-hop reaches 0.02 against 0.84 for
  2-hop. n (500 to 2,000) and rewiring (0.05 to 0.3) change nothing beyond noise.
- How credit is committed matters as much as hop count. The contract commits at `back`. If holders
  split their credit evenly ahead of demand, P falls from 0.50 to 0.12. Backing on request, or
  lines that commit on draw (a `back` and a borrow in one transaction), recovers it without any
  multi-hop routing. The even split is an assumption about behaviour, so this number brackets the
  deployed rule rather than predicting it.
- Load: at 100% offered load, success is 0.33, 0.64, 0.75, 0.89 and 0.96 across the five regimes.
  The unbounded network stays within 0.88 to 1.00 of the centralised lender at the base point,
  which agrees with Dandekar et al. (2011). One-hop stays at 0.26 to 0.43 of it.

### Where the gain comes from: 2 hops against more

Share of the gap between one-hop and unbounded that 2 hops and 3 hops close:

| Graph | fresh, 2 hops | steady, 2 hops | steady, 3 hops |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Watts-Strogatz | 96% | 71% | 93% |
| Barabasi-Albert | 100% | 82% | 99% |
| SBM, credit everywhere | 99% | 86% | 99% |
| SBM, credit in half the blocks | 52% | 36% | 68% |

- **When credit is spread, the second hop is most of the gain.** A borrower's neighbours almost
  always have a holder neighbour. The second hop turns the borrower's whole in-cut (degree x c =
  200) into capacity: the mean maximum rises from 40 to 163, against 201 for unbounded.
- **The third hop matters under load and on sparse graphs.** Repeated loans use up the credit
  within two hops. On the clustered Watts-Strogatz base graph, a borrower has 8 neighbours, about
  20 accounts at distance 2 and about 70 at distance 3, so the third hop reaches several times
  more holders. At degree 4 the steady-state
  rates are 0.49, 0.62 and 0.76 for 2-hop, 3-hop and unbounded.
- **When credit sits in other communities, long paths carry the gain.** Two hops close only a third
  to a half of the gap, and unbounded still adds 20 points over 3 hops. Those loans cross community
  boundaries (mean path 3.4 arcs), and every trust regime is then capped by the cut between
  communities. At q = 0.5 all credit sits in half the blocks and all borrowers in the other half.
  25,000 of demand must cross 220 arcs, 5,500 of capacity. The multi-hop regimes fall to 0.15 to
  0.22 and one-hop to 0.05, while the centralised lender stays at 1.

### Who bears a default

- **One-hop.** The direct backer's credit is burned (`creditLoss`), up to what it backed. It chose
  the borrower. Lenders bear the cash unless the backing is staked.
- **Multi-hop, default charged to the source** (M4, and the regime measured above). Each path's
  source holder has its credit burned. Every trust line on the path loses capacity. Intermediaries
  lose trust lines but no credit. Theorem 2 still holds, because every unit lent is a commitment of
  some holder's credit. The account that chose the borrower, though, often has nothing at stake.
  In the steady state at the base point (summary, section 2):
  - 50 to 83% of granted 2-hop loans needed a path of more than one arc;
  - 40 to 62% of 2-hop volume entered the borrower from an account with no credit;
  - 37 to 66% was charged to holders not adjacent to the borrower, who never chose it;
  - for unbounded routing these shares rise to 53 to 72% and 50 to 75%.
- **Multi-hop, default paid along the path** (Karlan et al. 2009). Each intermediary compensates the
  one before it, so the borrower's direct neighbour finally pays. On chain it can pay only from its
  credit or stake. That is the path-liable regime, and it is exactly one-hop:

  > **Proposition.** If every account must be able to pay, from its own free credit, for all it
  > passes on, the max-flow into a borrower equals the one-hop value.
  >
  > *Proof.* The flow on arc u -> b is at most c and at most u's throughput, which is at most
  > free(u). u can send min(c, free(u)) to b directly. Summing over b's neighbours gives the one-hop
  > value, and the one-hop allocation is itself feasible. The same argument covers a set of sinks.
  > It holds at every state, not only a fresh one. Checked on 2,580 sampled borrowers and in the
  > tests. ∎

  Karlan et al.'s intermediaries pay with social collateral, the value of their relationships. A
  contract over pseudonymous accounts cannot seize that. So **all of the multi-hop gain comes from
  letting accounts route credit they could not pay for themselves**, and charging the loss to a
  holder further up the path.
- **Centralised.** No trust relation selects the borrower. Lenders or the issuer bear the loss. A
  single Sybil can draw all free credit (20,000 at the base point).

### Sybil check

| Attack edges from | g | g x c | one-hop | 2-hop | 3-hop | unbounded | path-liable | unbounded charged to the endpoints |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| random accounts (5 hold credit) | 20 | 500 | 125 | 475 | 500 | 500 | 125 | 25% |
| random accounts (15 hold credit) | 50 | 1,250 | 375 | 1,075 | 1,250 | 1,250 | 375 | 34% |
| credit holders | 20 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 500 | 100% |
| accounts without credit | 20 | 500 | 0 | 400 | 500 | 500 | 0 | 0% |

These are Watts-Strogatz rows at m = 1. The values are identical at m = 10, 100 and 1,000.
`results/sybil.csv` has all 144 runs, on three graphs ([figure](figures/sybil_extraction.png)).

- **The bound survives.** In every trust regime, extraction equals the cut of the network the regime
  routes over. It is the same for m = 1 to 1,000 and never exceeds g x c. The centralised lender has
  no bound: extraction is all issued credit.
- **Its constant changes.** In one-hop only an attack edge from a credit holder is worth anything
  (c each), and that holder pays for it. In multi-hop every attack edge is worth up to c. An
  account with no credit can open one at no cost to itself. The loss lands on holders that never
  trusted a Sybil: 66 to 100% of it in the rows above whose endpoints are not all holders. With
  random endpoints, q = 0.2 and g >= 20, the same attack edges take 3.3 to 7 times as much under
  multi-hop as under one-hop, across the three graphs.
- The g x c bound in multi-hop also needs a default to consume the trust lines on its path, as M4
  and Karlan et al.'s lost links do. If a default burned only the source's credit, the same attack
  edge could carry fresh credit from other holders after every default. (This follows from the
  model; it is not simulated here.)

### Is multi-hop worth its on-chain cost?

- **Storage is not the obstacle.** Count the storage slots a loan touches, one per source and one
  per arc. A direct loan touches 5 to 7.5. A routed 2-hop loan touches 6 to 7, and a 3-hop loan 7 to 9.
  Unbounded routing touches 8 to 14, and 42 in the concentrated SBM, where paths are long. At
  roughly 5,000 to 22,100 gas per storage write, the difference is tens of thousands of gas per loan.
- **Computation moves off-chain.** The contract cannot compute a max-flow. The caller (the relayer
  routes in `packages/nextjs/app/api/meta/`) would submit a flow. The contract checks capacities
  and conservation in time linear in its arcs, and stores it to release on repayment and charge on
  default. Routes go stale when other loans draw first.
- **The real cost is the protocol change.** `back` would have to accept lines from accounts without
  credit; today it reverts with `InsufficientCredit`. Holders would then underwrite their
  neighbours' judgement of strangers.

**Verdict.**

- The one-hop rule costs about half of honest liquidity at realistic parameters, or two to four
  times the issued credit for the same service.
- Two hops recover 70 to 100% of that when credit is spread, at little on-chain cost. Paths of
  three or more hops pay only on sparse graphs, under heavy load, or when credit sits in other
  communities.
- The gain is not free in the currency the protocol cares about. It exists only because
  intermediaries can pass on credit they could not pay for. Section 5 of `CREDIT_MODEL.md` rests on
  the backer bearing the loss of its own choice, and multi-hop removes exactly that link.
- If multi-hop is added, two design choices keep its gain and make the liability explicit:
  - limit it to 2 hops;
  - make relaying a separate permission that the source grants per line. A holder that does not opt
    in keeps one-hop semantics. A holder that does underwrites its neighbour's choices knowingly.
- Committing backing on draw, rather than at `back`, is a cheaper first step. It keeps one-hop
  liability and can raise P(borrow 50) from 0.12 to 0.50 if holders would otherwise pre-commit
  evenly.

## Assumptions and limits

- **Network.** Trust is uniform (c on every edge) and symmetric, and every holder extends lines to
  all its neighbours. Real backers choose. One-hop numbers are an upper bound for the deployed
  contract, since a backer commits at `back`; multi-hop numbers assume every line can be relayed.
- **Credit.** Holders have equal lines and no stake, and do not borrow. Borrowers ask for a fixed 50
  and take all or nothing.
- **Steady state.** It has no defaults, because a default destroys capacity for good and then no
  steady state exists. Who bears a default is measured from the routes of granted loans.
- **Routing policy.** It is one reasonable policy: direct first, then fewest arc units (k = 2, 3)
  or shortest augmenting paths (unbounded). Other policies would shift steady-state rates a little.
- **Load.** The q sweep holds offered load at 50% of issued credit, so demand grows with q. The
  equivalence experiment holds each borrower's request rate fixed instead.
- **Not modelled.** The pool's cash (lenders never bind), interest, `MAX_BACKERS_PER_BORROWER`
  (32; it rarely binds at these degrees, but could for BA hubs), and the race between a backer
  cutting a line and a borrower drawing on it.
